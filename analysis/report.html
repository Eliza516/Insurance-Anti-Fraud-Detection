Detecting fraud in auto insurance claims

Data: insurance_claims.csv, repo Eliza516/Insurance-Anti-Fraud-Detection, commit 7d1b322 (originally from Kaggle, buntyshah). 1,000 claims, 40 columns. Full workings in analysis.ipynb.

Executive summary

  • A simple model catches about 86% of fraud cases. The best model (Naive Bayes, 10 features) correctly found 42 of 49 fraud cases in the held-out test set. About 6 in 10 flagged claims are actually fraudulent (precision 0.64), with F1 at 0.73 — versus F1 0.30 for a random guess at the base rate.
  • Two factors drive almost everything: "Major Damage" severity (60% fraud, vs. ~11% for other severities) and chess/cross-fit hobbies on the policy (~86% fraud).
  • A two-line rule matches the model almost exactly: "flag if Major Damage or hobby is chess/cross-fit." On the test set this rule agrees with the model's predictions 100% of the time.
  • Demographics carry no signal: age, gender, customer tenure, premium, and whether a police report was filed are all nearly identical between fraud and non-fraud claims.
  • Confidence in these numbers is moderate. The test set has only 49 fraud cases, so F1's 95% CI is 0.63–0.81. The data also looks simulated in places, so this should be validated against real claims before deployment.

1. Context & questions

The insurer needs to triage claims likely to be fraudulent before they reach adjusters. This report answers three questions:

  • How well do simple classifiers (KNN, Logistic Regression, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, Random Forest) detect fraud?
  • Which factors matter most (top 5)?
  • How does a decision tree explain the predictions?

2. Data

  • Scope & label: 1,000 incidents from Jan 1 to Mar 1, 2015, across 3 policy states. 247 claims (24.7%) are flagged fraudulent.
  • Quality: no duplicate rows. Column _c39 is entirely empty and was dropped. Missing values are coded as "?" in 3 columns: collision type (178 rows), property damage (360) and police report (343) — kept as an explicit "Unknown" category rather than dropped.
  • Columns excluded: identifiers and PII (policy number, zip code, incident location) aren't used as features.
  • Engineered features: policy age at the time of the incident, day of week, each claim type's share of the total, and vehicle age.
  • Caveats: small dataset, only two months of incidents. Some patterns (e.g. chess strongly predicting fraud) are hard to justify on business grounds, suggesting simulated data or labeling bias.

3. Data split

  • Split: 80% train (800 claims, 24.8% fraud), 20% test (200 claims, 24.5% fraud), matching the assignment's 8/2 requirement.
  • Why random: stratified by label to preserve the fraud rate. Each claim is an independent policy (checked for no overlap between splits), and this isn't a forecasting problem.
  • Model selection: models and hyperparameters chosen via 5-fold stratified CV on train. Test is touched exactly once, at the end.
  • Leakage control: every data-dependent step (scaling, encoding, feature selection) sits inside a pipeline, so it never sees test data.
  • Distribution check: a KS test on 8 key numeric features found no difference between train and test (all p > 0.1).

4. Key findings

4.1 Damage severity and hobbies are the strongest signals

  • Damage severity: in train, 60% of "Major Damage" claims are fraud, vs. 10% (Minor), 15% (Total Loss), 8% (Trivial).
  • Hobbies: chess and cross-fit hobbyists show 87% and 86% fraud rates; every other hobby is under 36%.
  • Statistical test: chi-square confirms both associations are strong (Cramér's V ≈ 0.50 and 0.46; p < 10-25).

What this means: combining the two signals splits claims into very different groups:

  • Not Major Damage, no chess/cross-fit hobby: only 4% fraud (529/800 claims).
  • Not Major Damage, chess/cross-fit hobby: 88% fraud.
  • Major Damage: 60% fraud.

This is a correlation in the data, not evidence that a hobby causes fraud.

4.2 Claim amounts are slightly higher for fraud

  • Median total claim is about $61,000 for fraud vs. $56,100 for non-fraud — statistically significant but a small effect.
  • Age, tenure and premium show no difference (p > 0.6).

4.3 Decision tree: how the model reasons

The tree's first two questions, in order:

  1. Is it Major Damage? If yes, it leans strongly toward fraud.
  2. If not: is the hobby chess?, then cross-fit?

Deeper branches split only on claim amounts and mostly land on the same conclusion either way — nearly all of the tree's logic sits in the first three questions.

Reading the results:

  • Confusion matrix: the model flags 66 claims, 42 of them real fraud. 7 fraud cases are missed.
  • Stability: test F1 (0.73) matches CV (0.755). Test PR-AUC (0.55) is lower than CV (0.72), but still inside the bootstrap 95% CI of 0.43–0.70.
  • Where the PR-AUC gap comes from: mostly the ranking within the flagged group, which relies on claim amounts — a weak signal — not the flag/no-flag decision itself.
  • Weak spot: the model flags every Major Damage claim (100% recall, 63% precision in that group). Outside it, recall drops to roughly a third (Total Loss) to a half (Minor Damage).

For reference (not used for model selection), test F1 at the default 0.5 threshold: Naive Bayes 0.74, Logistic Regression 0.73, Decision Tree 0.71, Random Forest 0.62, KNN 0.60.

6. Recommendations

  1. High priority: adopt the simple screening rule. Route every Major Damage claim and every chess/cross-fit hobby claim to review. On this data that catches ~86% of fraud while sending roughly a third of claims for review, and it's easy to explain to adjusters.
  2. High priority: validate against real claims before deploying. The hobby signal is unusual enough to warrant checking it isn't an artifact of how the data was simulated or labeled, and weighing the fairness risk of using personal-life details.
  3. Medium priority: pick a threshold by cost. 36% of flagged claims are currently false alarms. Estimate the cost of reviewing a claim against the cost of a missed fraud case, then set the threshold accordingly.
  4. Medium priority: improve detection on lower-severity claims. This is where the model misses the most. More data would help — claim history per customer, repair shop records, witness networks.

7. Limitations & next steps

  • Small sample: the test set has only 49 fraud cases, so scores are noisy (F1's CI spans 0.63–0.81).
  • Possibly simulated data: the signal concentrates too heavily on two variables, and the time window is short, so real-world generalization is uncertain.
  • Weak within-group ranking: the model doesn't distinguish well which Major Damage claims are more suspicious than others.
  • Next steps: collect more real data; run a cost-benefit analysis on the threshold; try combining the rule with a separate ranking model for the Major Damage group; check fairness across customer segments.
